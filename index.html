Why build this

Where do a market's preferences come from?

Mainstream economics models markets with game theory: agents with fixed preferences, optimising against one another inside a fixed set of rules. It is powerful — but it quietly assumes the very things a financial crisis is made of. It takes as given what people want, how much risk they will stomach, and how confident they feel. Those are exactly the things that shift as a boom builds.

The economist Hyman Minsky put this shifting at the centre of his Financial Instability Hypothesis: a long stretch of calm quietly convinces everyone that risk has gone away. Caution erodes, borrowing climbs, speculation spreads — until the market is a hair-trigger away from collapse. In his phrase, stability is destabilising. Minsky distrusted the tidy equations of game theory, so he left the idea vivid but mathematically loose.

This project takes a different foundation for the agents: Active Inference, a theory from cognitive science in which a mind is a prediction machine. It continually anticipates what it will sense, and — crucially — acts to make its predictions come true. Preferences are not fixed dials; they are downstream of what the agent has come to believe. Put a crowd of such agents in a market and you get preferences that are constructed — by experience, and by each other.

The wager of the model is simple: if you let belief do the work Minsky described, do boom-and-bust cycles appear on their own? They do. And with several markets to choose from, something extra appears — excess capital sloshing from one domain to the next, chasing the next story.

The model, plainly

One idea, four moving parts

Every trader is a small Active-Inference agent. It watches prices, infers whether each market is quietly appreciating, and places its capital accordingly — borrowing when it feels sure. Four design choices turn that into a Minsky world.

✦

Belief in appreciation is the driver

An agent's willingness to take on debt falls as its confidence that prices will keep rising grows. The engine of the boom is a belief the crowd talks itself into — and then confirms by buying.

◐

A memory of losses

Caution is not just belief. A path-dependent memory of losses — quick to spike, slow to fade — shades every agent's nerve, so fear lingers after a crash and confidence has to be rebuilt slowly.

⇄

Capital rotates between domains

With several markets, agents pour capital into whichever domain looks best. When its story sours, the money doesn't vanish — it displaces into the next promising domain.

⚖

Lopsided arbitrage

Bargain-hunters reliably buy a cheap asset (ending busts), but short-selling limits mean nobody firmly caps an expensive one — so bubbles are free to inflate. This asymmetry keeps cycles alive.

⌗

Minsky's ladder of fragility

Each agent is classed hedge (income covers its debts), speculative (covers only interest) or Ponzi (solvent only if prices keep rising) — the rungs Minsky described, here measured live.

◇

No two agents alike

Traders differ in nerve, patience and perception, so the crowd never moves in perfect lockstep — you get overlapping waves rather than one grand boom and a long silence.

Under the hood each agent is a discrete Active-Inference (POMDP) agent — inferring a hidden market regime, scoring where to put its capital by Expected Free Energy, and self-evidencing its own beliefs. You never need to read any of that: in the tool below, every one of these ideas is a labelled slider, and the “How it works” panel spells out the mathematics for the curious.

What the model shows

Four results you can reproduce below

70–95%capital concentration
Capital displaces between domains. With several markets, excess capital piles into one domain, then rotates to the next as beliefs shift — a visible, restless migration. The money never rests: watch the coloured bands trade places in the tool below. the headline result
+2 stepsbelief leads money
Belief leads the money. The crowd's belief in appreciation rises before aggregate leverage does — cross-correlation peaks at a positive lag (~2 steps, correlation ≈ 0.5). Confidence is the cause, not a coincident readout. The blue line moves first; the money follows.
1.5× → 1.1×peak bubble vs #domains
More domains, smaller bubbles. With a single market, prices spike to about 1.5× fundamental; give capital several places to flee and the peak falls toward 1.1×. Because excess money can always rotate elsewhere, the multi-domain structure acts as a pressure valve that damps any one bubble — a genuinely emergent effect.
a dialnot a law of nature
The crash is a setting. Turn up the crowd's aversion to debt (“Cautious crowd”) and average leverage falls from ~1.2× toward ~0.9× — the market calms. Turn it down (“Euphoria”) and borrowing climbs. Fragility isn't fixed; it responds to how the agents are disposed — Minsky's point, made into a lever you can pull.
