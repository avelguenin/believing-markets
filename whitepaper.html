1Abstract & scope

We present a computational agent-based model in which financial boom-and-bust, and the migration of capital between markets, arise endogenously from the beliefs of cognitive agents rather than from fixed preferences or exogenous shocks.

Each trader is a discrete Active-Inference agent (a partially-observable Markov decision process, POMDP). It infers, per market, a hidden "regime" from observed price movements, and allocates its — possibly borrowed — capital toward markets it believes are appreciating. Its willingness to hold debt is not a parameter but a function of that belief. From this we recover the qualitative content of Hyman Minsky's Financial Instability Hypothesis (FIH): tranquillity erodes caution, leverage accumulates, and financing structures shade from robust to fragile. Extending the model to several markets ("domains") produces a second, emergent phenomenon: excess capital displaces from one domain to another as beliefs shift, and this rotation measurably damps the amplitude of any single bubble.

This is a stylized, mechanistic model, not a calibrated or predictive one. All quantitative results below are properties of the specified equations at the stated parameters and random seeds; none is an empirical claim about real markets. Section 12 states precisely what is and is not asserted.

2Theoretical motivation

2.1 The closure problem in economic theory

Game theory, since von Neumann & Morgenstern (1944), is the standard apparatus for microeconomics because it characterises the equilibria and dynamics of many agents interacting under complex conventions. Its analytical power, however, rests on taking two things as given: the system of conventions the agents inhabit, and the preferences they hold within it. Both are exactly what an evolving economy — and, acutely, a financial crisis — tends to remake. The point is not that agents are irrational but that the model is closed at the wrong place: the objects a theory of instability most needs to explain (what agents want, how much risk they will bear, how confident they feel) are entered as fixed parameters. Critics from several traditions have pressed this — that economic modelling brackets the creative evolution of the very preferences and conventions it studies (Chavalarias, 2009; Felin, Kauffman, Koppl & Longo, 2014) — but no standard method endogenises them, because doing so forfeits the tidy input–output laws that make a model tractable. The result is a structural blind spot precisely where financial fragility lives.

2.2 Minsky's Financial Instability Hypothesis

Hyman Minsky, trained in the evolutionary tradition under Schumpeter, placed the endogeneity of fragility at the centre of his account of capitalist finance (Minsky, 1982). His Financial Instability Hypothesis (FIH) holds that a prolonged tranquil expansion is self-undermining: as memories of loss fade and asset values validate past optimism, participants revise their perceived risk downward, accept thinner margins of safety, and shift the composition of financing. Minsky named three postures on a ladder of fragility — hedge units, whose income covers principal and interest; speculative units, whose income covers only interest and which must therefore roll over debt; and Ponzi units, whose income covers neither and which remain solvent only while asset prices continue to rise. As an expansion matures the population drifts up this ladder, so the system becomes progressively more exposed to any interruption of the upward trend — "stability is destabilising." The 2008 crisis renewed the hypothesis's prominence, yet Minsky's own methodological hostility to game-theoretic formalism left it mathematically underdetermined, and later modellers have not converged on a canonical translation (Nikolaidi, 2017; Nikolaidi & Stockhammer, 2018). The FIH is thus a phenomenon in search of a foundation: a qualitative account of endogenous instability without an agreed micro-mechanism generating it.

2.3 Active Inference and the endogeneity of preference

Active Inference (Friston et al., 2010; Parr, Pezzulo & Friston, 2022), the process-theoretic reading of the Free Energy Principle, supplies a candidate foundation because of how it represents an agent. A cognitive system is modelled as minimising a variational bound on the surprise of its sensory and active states: it maintains posterior beliefs about the hidden causes of its sensations and selects actions expected to yield its preferred observations. Three features of this construction matter here. First, perception and action are unified under one imperative, so an agent both infers the market and acts so as to confirm its inferences — the "self-evidencing" character of active inference (Hohwy, 2016), which supplies a natural micro-account of the self-fulfilling dynamics central to bubbles. Second, preferences are priors: they are encoded as prior expectations over outcomes rather than as a separate utility functional, so preferences and beliefs live in the same representational currency and either can be made to depend on the other. (Under information-theoretic bounds on rationality the objective of such an agent coincides, in the fully-observed case, with that of an expected-utility maximiser, so this is a generalisation of, not a departure from, the standard decision-theoretic core.) Third, because preferences are representational states, they can be made adaptive and socially coupled — updated by an agent's own experience and pulled toward the beliefs of others. This is what lets "the social construction of preferences" be stated as a mechanism rather than a slogan: if the preference against debt is a function of a belief, and beliefs spread through a population, then preferences are literally built by experience and by the crowd.

2.4 The modelling question

These strands motivate a single, sharply-posed question, testable within a model: if an agent's preference against debt is made a function of its own inferred belief in continued appreciation — and nothing else is assumed irrational or exogenously shocked — do Minsky-type dynamics follow? Answering it requires committing to specific mechanics the source abstract leaves open; the remainder of this document specifies those commitments (§§3–9) and reports what the resulting system does (§10) and what that establishes (§11).

3Relation to the source paper

The source abstract specifies (i) a framework — Active Inference; (ii) a target — the FIH; (iii) two ingredients: "a basic financial market where agents may borrow at a given rate … as well as broadcast and accept offers for buying / selling a … collection of [assets]" and "an assembly of computational agents implemented in PyMDP who can observe and engage with this financial market, as well as update their preferences adaptively to avoid bankruptcy and maximize the value of their holdings"; and (iv) a conjecture: "Minsky cycles will naturally emerge, as prolonged market stability will lead agents to diminish their preference against debt and therefore adopt riskier investment strategies." It also poses an open question: whether "additional structure (such as explicit account of social interaction and emotional dynamics) will be necessary."

Everything below the italicised specification is a modelling decision made here, documented so each can be contested. The two most consequential are: (a) the preference against debt is driven by the agent's own inferred belief in appreciation rather than a separate accumulator (Section 5); and (b) the model is extended from one asset to several to study capital displacement (Section 8).

4Generative model (per agent, per domain)

Each agent carries an independent generative model for every domain k. All agents share the same model structure and likelihood/transition matrices; they differ in their posteriors, in temperament parameters (Section 5.4), and in a per-agent perception bias.

4.1 Hidden state and observations

  • Hidden state — market regime s ∈ {Bull, Neutral, Bear}, inferred, never observed directly.
  • Observation — price move oP ∈ {Up, Flat, Down}, generated by the domain's realised return.
  • Observation — wealth outcome oW ∈ {Gain, Even, Loss}, generated by the agent's realised portfolio return (shared across the agent's domain-models; see §5).

The single-asset variant (§6.3) additionally carries a controllable position factor {Defensive, Flat, Levered}; in the deployed multi-domain model position is replaced by a continuous capital allocation (§6).

4.3 Preferences

Preferences are prior log-expectations over the wealth observation, C = [ +greed, 0, −γ ] over {Gain, Even, Loss}. greed is fixed (default 1.5); the loss weight γ (gammaLoss) is the adaptive quantity that carries Minsky's mechanism (§5).

5Perception and the preference against debt

5.1 State inference

Each step, each domain-belief is updated by one step of Bayesian filtering — a temporal prior from the transition matrix, multiplied by the two observation likelihoods, then normalised:

beliefk ← normalize( (B · beliefk) ⊙ AP[·, oPk] ⊙ AW[Flat, ·, oW] ) # ⊙ = elementwise

The wealth likelihood is evaluated at the Flat row: per-domain regime inference uses the domain's own price move plus the agent's overall wealth outcome as evidence. (Using the global wealth outcome for every domain is a simplification; §12.)

5.2 Belief in appreciation

The scalar read off each posterior is the belief the domain will appreciate — the quantity a Minsky "Ponzi" unit implicitly bets on:

pAppk = clamp( ( beliefk[Bull] − beliefk[Bear] + 1 ) / 2 , 0, 1 )

5.3 Caution → loss aversion (the core coupling)

The preference against debt is a decreasing function of the agent's confidence in its best opportunity, blended with a path-dependent emotional memory (§5.4):

bestApp = maxk pAppk caution = clamp( ( (1 − bestApp) + emotion · scar ) / (1 + emotion) , 0, 1 ) γ = gammaFloor + (gammaCeil − gammaFloor) · caution # γ ∈ [0.4, 6.0]

High confidence in some domain ⇒ low caution ⇒ low γ ⇒ the agent tolerates debt; a collapse of belief restores caution. This is the paper's conjecture, made mechanical.

5.4 Emotional memory and heterogeneity

Each agent carries a scalar scar ∈ [0,1], an asymmetric exponential moving average of loss experience — fear spikes quickly, confidence rebuilds slowly:

if oW = Loss : scar ← scar + shock·shockMul·(1 − scar) # fast spike else : scar ← scar − decay·decayMul·scar # slow recovery

Agents are heterogeneous: per-agent multipliers decayMul, shockMul ∈ [0.6, 1.4], allocation-precision multiplier precMul ∈ [0.7, 1.3], a perception bias percNoise ∈ ±0.006 added to the perceived return, and an initial scar ∈ [0.2, 0.8]. This desynchronises the population, producing overlapping waves rather than one global boom.

5.5 Social contagion

After the individual updates, each agent's per-domain posterior is nudged toward the population mean posterior, so beliefs — and hence, through §5.3, preferences — are constructed socially:

beliefk ← normalize( beliefk + social · ( mean_beliefk − beliefk ) ) # social = 0.08

6Action: the capital-allocation policy

6.1 Per-domain appeal

For each domain the agent forms the expected preference-weighted valence of investing there, under its predicted next-step regime and the levered likelihood (the opportunity assessment):

o = AW[Levered, ·] · (B · beliefk) appealk = greed · o[Gain] − γ · o[Loss]

Relation to Expected Free Energy. appeal is the pragmatic (goal-directed) term of Expected Free Energy — the expected value of outcomes relative to preferences. The deployed multi-domain policy uses this term only. The single-asset variant (§6.3) additionally includes the epistemic (ambiguity-avoiding) term and an explicit debt prior, and selects among discrete positions. The multi-domain rule is therefore Active-Inference-derived but is a reduced, one-step, epistemics-free approximation, adopted for tractability across many domains; it is not a full EFE-minimising planner.

6.2 Destination policy, exposure and leverage

A softmax over destinations {cash, domain₁…domainK} sets how capital is split; overall exposure scales with the risk-on share and is discounted by caution:

w = softmax( allocPrecision · precMul · [ cashBias, appeal1, …, appealK ] ) riskyShare = 1 − w[cash] m = defensiveMult + (1 + maxLeverage − defensiveMult) · riskyShare if m > 1 : m = 1 + (m − 1) / (1 + debtAversion · γ) # caution gates leverage equity = cash + Σk qk·Pk − debt V = m · equity # total desired risky value desiredQk = V · ( w[k] / Σj w[j] ) / Pk

Positions cannot be reached instantly — a partial adjustment moves only a fraction toward target each step, Δqk = adjustSpeed · (desiredQk − qk) with adjustSpeed = 0.28. This friction is what allows fire-sales to trap leverage and produce clustered insolvencies (§7.4).

6.3 Single-asset EFE variant

For one domain the model uses the fuller decision. For each position a ∈ {Defensive, Flat, Levered} with predicted regime ŝ = B·belief and predicted wealth obs o = AW[a]·ŝ:

risk = Σo o · ( ln o − C ) ambiguity = Σs ŝ · H[ AW[a, ·, s] ] G(a) = risk + ambiguityWeight · ambiguity + debtAversion · γ · 𝟙[a = Levered] a ~ softmax( − precision · G )

i.e. Expected Free Energy with an explicit prior against the indebted state, minimised stochastically. Results for this variant are reported separately in §10.6.

7The market

7.1 Fundamental value and income

Each domain's asset pays a fixed cash dividend per unit per step, so a fundamental value exists: F = dividendCash / discount = 0.6 / 0.006 = 100. Because income is fixed in cash, a high price implies a low yield, so an over-priced domain cannot service debt from income — fragility is endogenous.

7.2 Price formation

Each domain clears against a single reduced-form market-maker that absorbs residual demand. The one-step return combines demand pressure, fundamentalist arbitrage, a convex ceiling, and noise:

excessk = Σagents Δqk pressure = tanh( excessk · K / N ) # depth ∝ 1/K: each domain is 1/K of capital gap = ln( F / Pk ) revert = ( gap > 0 ? meanRev : meanRev·revAsym ) · gap # ASYMMETRIC arbitrage over = max(0, Pk/F − 1) drag = − bubbleDrag · over² / (1 + over) # CONVEX bubble ceiling ret = clamp( priceImpact·pressure + revert + drag + noise , −0.35, 0.35 ) Pk ← max( Pk·(1 + ret) , 10⁻³ )

Two design conditions here are load-bearing (see §10.4, §10.6):

  • Asymmetric arbitrage (revAsym = 0.25): buying pressure on a cheap asset is four times stronger than the drag on an expensive one, reflecting short-sale limits. Without it the market is bistable — it locks into permanent slump or permanent bubble depending only on the seed. With it, both booms and busts self-terminate and cycles recur.
  • Convex ceiling (bubbleDrag = 0.015): negligible near F, firm far above it, so bubbles form freely yet are bounded at a few × F instead of diverging.

7.3 Accounting

Per agent, per step, in order: fill Δqk at the new price (borrowing if cash is exhausted); receive dividends Σ dividendCash·qk; pay interest interest·debt (capitalised if unfunded); repay debt with any spare cash; mark equity to market. The agent's realised portfolio return (plus its perception bias) determines its wealth observation via thresholds gainThresh = 0.015, lossThresh = −0.018.

7.4 Insolvency

If equity turns negative the agent is liquidated and re-enters as a fresh, maximally cautious participant (scar = 1, pessimistic beliefs, cash restored to the initial endowment). Population is thus held fixed; wealth is not conserved (§12).

7.5 Minsky financing classes

Each indebted agent is classified from income against debt service, with interestDue = interest·debt and principalDue = amortize·debt (amortize = 0.04):

Ponzi if income < interestDue # solvent only on capital gains Speculative if interestDue ≤ income < interestDue+principalDue Hedge otherwise (or debt ≈ 0)

8Multi-domain dynamics & capital displacement

With K > 1, the allocation softmax (§6.2) concentrates each agent's capital in the domain whose belief is currently strongest. As that belief matures — the domain stops rising, its price observations turn Flat, its appeal decays — a neglected domain, now cheap and recovering under asymmetric arbitrage, becomes relatively attractive, and capital rotates. The money is not destroyed at a downturn; it displaces.

Because concentrated demand in a shallow domain (depth ∝ 1/K) can move price, this rotation is also a coupling between domains: capital fleeing a souring market bleeds pressure out of it and inflates the next. The measured consequence (§10.4) is that more domains reduce the peak amplitude of any single bubble — the multi-domain structure behaves as a pressure valve.

10Experimental conditions & results

Method. Deterministic seeded runs (mulberry32 PRNG). Unless noted: 2500 steps, the first 200 discarded as transient, statistics over the remainder, averaged over seeds {5, 2024, 42, 7, 99, 3}. 10.2 Belief leads leverage

Cross-correlating the crowd's mean appreciation belief with aggregate leverage, the peak occurs at a positive lag of +2 to +3 steps (correlation 0.23–0.52 across seeds): belief moves first, borrowing follows. Belief is the driver, not a coincident readout. single-asset variant: lag +4 to +5

10.3 Capital displacement

At K = 3 the share of all capital held in the single largest domain averages ≈49% and peaks ≈76% (against 33% under a uniform split), and rotates between domains over time. Concentration rises as domains are removed or focus sharpened: at K = 2 the peak single-domain share reaches ≈92%. This restless concentration-and-rotation is the model's headline multi-domain behaviour.

10.7 Robustness and performance

No non-finite or degenerate (price ≤ 0) trajectories were observed across parameter extremes tested individually: agents 15–400, domains 1–6, interest 0–5%/step, leverage 0.2–4×, allocation precision 1–6, social 0–0.6, emotion 0–1, mean-reversion 0, noise up to 0.03, price impact up to 0.15. Throughput is ≈5,400 steps/s at K = 3, N = 120 in a single browser thread, so the model runs in real time.

11What the model establishes

The results of §10 are properties of a specific simulation. Their interest is conceptual, and it is worth stating precisely — and modestly — what building and running the model does and does not show.

11.1 A how-possibly demonstration: Minsky without irrationality or shocks

The model is, in the philosophy-of-science sense, a how-possibly explanation: it exhibits a mechanism sufficient to produce the target phenomenon, without claiming that mechanism is the one operating in real markets. What it establishes is an existence result. Minsky-type dynamics — accumulating leverage in calm, a drift up the hedge → speculative → Ponzi ladder, and cyclical fragility — arise here from agents that are Bayesian and boundedly rational, with no exogenous shock, no irrationality, and no behavioural rule imposed by hand. The only substantive assumption is that the preference against debt is a function of an inferred belief. This matters because a common objection to the FIH is that it must smuggle in either myopia or an outside trigger; the model shows that neither is necessary. Endogenous instability follows from the coupling of belief-driven leverage to a self-evidencing market. (It also shows the converse in the single-domain limit: with the coupling but without a bounding structure the dynamics do not merely cycle — they diverge, §10.6, so the FIH's "recurring cycle" is a property of the whole system, not of the belief loop alone.)

11.2 Preferences as beliefs: a representational unification

Operationalising the preference against debt as 1 − (belief in appreciation) collapses two descriptions that are usually separate. In standard treatments a Ponzi borrower is defined financially — by a cash-flow relation (income below interest, solvent only on capital gains). Here that same agent is defined cognitively — it is one whose posterior places high mass on the appreciating regime and whose caution has accordingly relaxed. The financial category and the cognitive state are the same object viewed from two sides. This is the concrete cash-value of "preferences are priors" (§2.3): it lets the "social construction of preferences" be a mechanism (belief contagion changes the caution that sets leverage), and it makes the model's central quantity — belief in appreciation — both the thing agents infer and the thing that drives the aggregate. The measured lead of belief over leverage (§10.2) is the signature of that direction of causation within the model.

11.3 The necessity — and multiplicity — of a bounding structure

The source abstract asks whether "additional structure (such as … emotional dynamics)" is necessary. The model returns a sharper, two-part answer. Some bounding structure is necessary: an unbounded belief–leverage loop is explosively unstable (§10.6, single-domain). But the identity of the binding structure is not unique. This implementation supplies three candidates — a lopsided fundamentalist arbitrage, a convex price ceiling, and, with several markets, capital rotation — and finds that an emotional loss-memory is decisive only in the single-domain case, being largely redundant once the others are present. The theoretical upshot is that Minsky's "emotional" story and a purely structural (arbitrage/liquidity) story are not rival explanations of boundedness but substitutable ones; the FIH does not privilege a psychological brake. This is a genuine, if negative, contribution to the modelling debate the abstract situates itself in.

11.4 Capital displacement: an emergent prediction beyond the original programme

Extending the framework from one market to several yields a phenomenon not contained in the single-asset conjecture: excess capital does not simply inflate and deflate a bubble in place, it migrates between domains as relative beliefs shift (§10.3), and this migration is not neutral — it damps the peak amplitude of any one bubble (§10.4), because a souring domain can always shed pressure into a fresher one. This is an emergent, qualitative prediction of the cognitive framing: it says that in a world of many competing "stories" for capital, systemic fragility may express itself less as a single towering bubble than as a restless rotation of moderate ones — a different empirical fingerprint from the classic single-asset FIH. Whether real capital rotation exhibits this damping is an empirical question the model raises but does not settle; its contribution is to make the prediction precise and mechanistically grounded.

11.5 Methodological contribution and epistemic status

Methodologically, the model is a worked instance of cognitively-grounded macro-finance: it closes the economic model at a different place (§2.1) by deriving preferences from the same inferential machinery that governs perception and action, rather than positing them. That closure is the point — it turns "where do the shifting preferences come from?" from an assumption into a mechanism. What the model does not establish must be equally clear: it is not calibrated, fits no data, and forecasts nothing; its parameters are illustrative and its numbers hold for the stated seeds. It is a demonstration of conceptual sufficiency and a generator of qualitative hypotheses, not a validated account of any actual market. Its value is to show that a specific, plausible cognitive assumption is enough to reproduce a family of macro-financial regularities — and to make the assumptions, and their consequences, fully explicit and manipulable. Section 12 details the boundaries of these claims.

12Limitations & scope of claims

  • What the agents are. Discrete POMDP agents performing one-step Bayesian belief updates and preference-weighted action selection. "Cognition" here denotes exactly that machinery — not biological, phenomenal, or human-equivalent cognition. No such stronger claim is made or implied.
  • Reduced decision rule. The deployed multi-domain policy uses only the pragmatic term of Expected Free Energy, over a single step, with no epistemic (information-seeking) term and no multi-step planning. It is Active-Inference-derived, not a full EFE-minimising planner. The single-asset variant is closer to canonical EFE but still one-step.
  • Stylized, not calibrated. Parameters are illustrative and were tuned for qualitative behaviour and numerical robustness, not estimated against data. No result is a forecast or an empirical claim about real markets; each is a property of these equations at these settings.
  • Market microstructure. A single infinitely-elastic market-maker per domain absorbs residual demand; there is no order book, bid–ask spread, or explicit liquidity provider. Price impact and the 1/K depth scaling are reduced-form.
  • Non-conservation. Dividends and interest are exogenous flows and the market-maker absorbs residual cash, so aggregate wealth is not conserved; insolvency respawns agents to hold population fixed.
  • Coupled beliefs. Per-domain regime inference is fed by the agent's global portfolio wealth observation, which couples domain beliefs more than a per-domain P&L signal would.
  • Nonlinearity and sensitivity. The system is nonlinear and can behave qualitatively differently near boundaries (e.g. the engine-dependent emotion result, §10.6). Reported numbers hold for the stated seeds and parameterisation; they are illustrative of mechanisms, not universal constants.
  • Not advice. This is a research and teaching model. It is not financial advice and not a validated macro-financial model.

13Reproducibility

The entire model and interface are a single self-contained HTML file with no external dependencies; the simulation runs client-side. Randomness is a seeded mulberry32 generator: one stream for market noise and initial heterogeneity, per-agent streams for the single-asset variant's stochastic action selection. A given (seed, parameters) pair therefore reproduces bit-for-bit. Results above use a fixed warm-up of 200 steps and the seed set stated in §10. All matrices, thresholds and defaults are given in Sections 4–9; nothing further is required to reconstruct the model.
